The method

A study system that actually works.

Not tips. A system. The order you do things in matters more than how many hours you put in — here's the order, why it works, and how it changes between Grade 9 and Grade 10.

Foundations

The core philosophy

Content before papers

Learn the material first, then practise exam technique. Doing past papers on a topic you don't understand yet just teaches you to feel bad. Understand it, then test it.

The syllabus is your only curriculum

Everything the exam can ask is in the syllabus. Everything. Nothing more, nothing less. If it's not in the syllabus, it can't be examined — so stop revising it. Build your notes topic-by-topic straight from the syllabus contents.

Testing over re-reading

Re-reading feels like learning. It isn't. The feeling of "I know this" when you look at your notes is recognition, not recall. The only thing that counts in the exam is whether you can produce the answer on a blank page under pressure. So test yourself constantly — close the book and write.

Two years is an asset, not a burden

You have time almost no one uses well. Grade 9 is for understanding without pressure. Grade 10 is for turning that understanding into marks. Don't try to do Grade 10's job in Grade 9 — and don't waste Grade 9 pretending exams are far away.

Genuinely different advice

Grade 9 vs Grade 10

These two years need different strategies. Doing Grade 10's routine in Grade 9 burns you out; doing Grade 9's routine in Grade 10 leaves you under-practised. Here's each.

The goal

Build genuine understanding of content — not exam technique yet. This is the year you learn the actual material properly, without the pressure of timed papers.

End-of-year target

Your tracker is mostly Amber / Green for the topics you've covered so far. Not everything — just what you've been taught.

  • Use the syllabus to structure your notes — one topic at a time, in the order your class covers them.
  • After finishing a chapter, do topic-specific past-paper questions on just that chapter — not full papers. Untimed, notes allowed at first.
  • Set up your tracker in September and update it every 3 weeks, not every session.
  • Don't do full timed papers yet. You haven't covered enough content — a full paper would mostly test topics you haven't been taught.
  • Focus on movement: turning Red topics Amber, Amber topics Green, slowly and honestly.
The goal

Convert understanding into exam marks. You know the content now — this year is about technique, timing, and the language mark schemes reward.

The final 6 weeks

Only past papers and targeted revision of Red topics. No new note-making, no re-reading things you already know cold. Attack weaknesses.

  • Full timed past papers, one per subject every 2–3 weeks from Term 1. Real conditions.
  • Mark-scheme reading becomes a conscious skill you practise — not just a way to get a number.
  • Read the examiner report for every paper you attempt.
  • Maintain a keyword bank actively, and review it monthly.
  • Treat mocks (usually Feb/March 2028) like the real thing — same prep, same nerves, same review afterwards.
  • After mocks, target your weakest papers specifically. Don't re-do the ones you're already good at because they feel nice.
Self-assessment

The traffic-light system

A simple self-assessment grid — straight from the Cambridge syllabus — that tells you, honestly, what you actually know. The whole tracker is built on it.

Red

I don't understand this yet, or I couldn't answer a question on it. Needs teaching or serious revision.

Amber

I understand it, but I'm not reliable under exam conditions. Some gaps, some hesitation.

Green

I can answer past-paper questions on this correctly, from a blank page, with no notes.

The one rule that makes this work

A topic is only Green after you've answered past-paper questions on it correctly, without notes. Not after reading it. Not after making pretty notes. Not after "feeling like you get it." After testing. If you skip this rule, your tracker turns into a wall of green lies and you'll walk into the exam over-confident.

How to update it: every 2–3 weeks, not every study session. Ratings should reflect a real trend, not how you feel on one good or bad day.

Where it lives: your tracker — load a syllabus and it builds the whole grid for you.

Decode the question

Command words — the full table

The first word of a question tells you exactly what to do. Answering the wrong command word is the single most common way students lose marks they'd otherwise earn. Underline the command word before you write anything.

Command wordWhat Cambridge meansDepthMost common mistakeMarks hint
State / Name / Give / IdentifyOne clear factual point. No explanation.LowOver-explaining — wastes time, earns nothing extra.1 mark each
DefinePrecise technical definition. Use syllabus language.LowVague everyday language. Cambridge is strict here.1–2
DescribeWhat / how. The facts of what happens or what it looks like.MidAnswering "why" instead of "what." Describe ≠ Explain.2–4
ExplainWhy / because. Link cause to effect explicitly.MidDescribing without the cause-effect chain. Vague statements get partial credit at best.2–6
SuggestApply knowledge to the specific scenario. No single right answer, but must be relevant and reasoned.MidGeneric rote recall not connected to the scenario given.2–4
Calculate / Find / DetermineShow all working. Units required. Method marks available even if the final answer is wrong.MidMissing units, skipped steps, wrong significant figures.2–4
CompareSimilarities AND differences, explicitly linked. Use comparative language.MidListing A's features then B's features separately — that's not a comparison.2–6
DeduceReach a conclusion from given evidence or data.Mid–HighMaking a claim without showing what evidence led to it.2–4
AnalyseBreak into components, show how they relate. Structured reasoning.HighDescribing or listing without the analytical layer.4–8
DiscussMultiple perspectives or factors, developed. Often expects both sides.HighOne-sided answers. Undeveloped points.6–12
EvaluateWeigh evidence, reach a justified conclusion that follows from your argument.HighRestating earlier points as "evaluation." A conclusion that doesn't follow from the argument.6–12
JustifyGive reasons that support a decision or conclusion.HighAsserting without reasoning.3–6

Remember it long-term

Spaced repetition, applied to IGCSE

The science in one line: you remember things longer if you review them at increasing intervals, just as you're about to forget. You don't need an app or a theory — your traffic-light tracker already is a spaced-repetition system if you use it by colour:

Red

Revisit every week. These are the topics costing you marks — they get the most attention.

Amber

Revisit every 2 weeks. Almost there — enough contact to push them to Green.

Green

Revisit once a month to maintain. Don't let solid topics rot from neglect.

Flashcards

For definitions and keyword-bank terms, flashcards (Anki or plain paper) are perfect — they're pure recall, which is exactly what the exam tests. Keep them for the small, exact things: definitions, formulae, key terms. Not for whole essay plans.

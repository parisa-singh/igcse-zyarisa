Economics

A subject that looks like reading and reasoning, but the marks live in the diagrams and the data. Draw it right, label it right, and actually use the numbers you're given — that's most of the battle.

The subject

What Economics is, at IGCSE

IGCSE Economics starts from one idea — the basic economic problem, that resources are scarce but wants are unlimited — and builds outward from there. You'll look at how resources get allocated through microeconomics and markets (supply, demand, price, competition), then zoom out to the wider economy (government policy, employment, inflation, growth) and finally to international trade and how countries depend on each other. It's less about long essays from memory and more about reading a diagram or a set of data and reasoning your way through it — so diagrams and data interpretation sit at the centre of nearly everything you'll be marked on.

Where marks leak

Command-word pitfalls in Economics

"Analyse"

Don't just describe what happens — link cause and effect through the market or the economy. Trace the chain: this changes, which changes that, which changes this. A list of effects with no links between them loses marks.

"Discuss" / "Evaluate"

Both of these want both sides — the case for and the case against — followed by a judgement. That judgement has to be justified, not just asserted, and it needs to show you understand the limitations or qualifications of your own argument, not just pick a side and stop.

"Calculate"

Work it out from the data given, show every step of your working, and give the answer with the right unit — a percentage, a dollar sign, whatever the question calls for. An answer with no working and no unit rarely gets full marks even if the number is right.

The specific stuff

Economics tips that actually move marks

Diagrams carry the paper

Economics is heavily diagram-based — supply and demand curves, PPCs, and more. Every diagram must be fully and correctly labelled, and you must actually refer to it in your written answer. A perfect diagram that your writing never mentions doesn't earn the marks it should.

Paper 1: eliminate, don't guess blind

When you're not sure of the answer, use process of elimination — rule out the options you know are wrong before choosing between what's left. It turns a guess into an educated one.

Data response: use the data

Always refer directly to the data you've been given — quote actual figures, not just general points. "Unemployment rose" is weaker than "unemployment rose from 4% to 7%." The numbers are there to be used.

Evaluation needs nuance

Show awareness of limitations, qualifications, and alternative perspectives — not just a tidy conclusion. "It depends on..." followed by a real reason is often exactly what the mark scheme is looking for.

Correlation isn't causation

In data questions it's tempting to say one thing caused another just because they moved together — but the data on its own doesn't prove that. If prices rose and sales fell in the same period, don't assume the price rise caused the fall unless you can actually justify the link. Examiners notice this mistake, so flag it as a limitation instead of stating it as fact.

The subject

What English Literature is, at IGCSE

IGCSE Literature is close study of poetry, prose and drama — set texts you'll read again and again until you know them properly, not skim once and hope. There's no tiering here, everyone sits the same papers. What examiners are actually looking for is personal, well-supported interpretation: your own reading of a text, backed by evidence from it. What they are not looking for is plot summary. Retelling what happens in a scene earns you almost nothing — showing how a writer builds an effect, and what it means, is where every mark lives.

Where marks leak

Command-word pitfalls in Literature

"Explore" / "How does the writer..."

Both are asking the same thing: analyse the writer's methods and the effects they create — never just what happens. Every point should be rooted in the actual text: a word, an image, a structural choice, a line of dialogue. If you can't point to it on the page, it isn't evidence yet.

Analyse

The chain is technique → effect → meaning, and it needs to be developed, not just named. Spotting a metaphor gets you nothing on its own — explaining what it does to the reader, and why the writer chose it there, is where the marks are.

Avoid narrating the plot

Markers want interpretation supported by detail, not a retelling of "what happens next." If a sentence could be written by someone who'd only read a summary of the text, cut it and replace it with something only close reading could produce.

The specific stuff

Literature tips that actually move marks

Essay technique: depth over breadth

Literature is marked on the quality of your analysis, not the quantity of points you cram in. Four ideas explored properly will always outscore ten ideas mentioned once each. Go deep on fewer things.

Embed your quotations

Don't list quotations like evidence in a courtroom — embed short ones directly into your own sentences, then analyse the specific words inside them. "Weave and zoom in" beats "quote and move on" every time.

Context vs close reading

Context earns marks only where it genuinely illuminates the text — never as a bolted-on fact. Close reading of the actual language on the page is always the priority; context is the seasoning, not the meal.

Command words share one demand

"Explore", "Analyse", "How does the writer..." — in a literature context they're all asking for the same thing: methods and their effects, argued from the text, not around it.
